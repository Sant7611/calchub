Body Mass Index (BMI) is the most widely used first-pass screen for whether a
person's weight is in a healthy range for their height. Doctors, insurers and
fitness apps all quote it — yet most people have never seen the actual
calculation. It takes ten seconds.

## The formula

BMI is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres, squared:

`BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height² (m²)`

That's the entire formula. No age, no gender, no body-fat measurement — just
two numbers.

## A worked example

Take someone who weighs **70 kg** and is **1.75 m** tall:

1. Square the height: 1.75 × 1.75 = **3.0625**
2. Divide the weight by that: 70 ÷ 3.0625 = **22.9**

A BMI of 22.9 lands squarely in the "normal weight" band.

If your measurements are in other units, convert first: 1 kg ≈ 2.2046 lb and
1 m ≈ 3.2808 ft. The [BMI Calculator](/tools/health/bmi-calculator) accepts
metric inputs and shows your category instantly.

## The WHO categories

The World Health Organization's adult classification is:

- **Underweight** — below 18.5
- **Normal weight** — 18.5 to 24.9
- **Overweight** — 25.0 to 29.9
- **Obesity** — 30.0 and above

These bands are population-level screening thresholds, not diagnostic
verdicts. A BMI of 26 is a prompt to look closer — at waist measurement,
activity, blood work — not a label.

<Callout title="South Asian cut-offs">
Several health bodies use lower thresholds for South Asian populations
because cardiometabolic risk rises at lower BMI: overweight from 23 and
obesity from 25. If you are from Nepal, India or the region, mention this to
your doctor — a "normal" WHO number can still warrant attention.
</Callout>

## What BMI gets right

- **It is consistent.** Two numbers, one formula, no equipment — so trends
  over months and years are comparable.
- **It correlates.** At a population level, higher BMI tracks with higher
  rates of type-2 diabetes, hypertension and cardiovascular disease.
- **It is free.** No lab, no scan, no subscription — which is exactly why it
  became the global default screen.

## What BMI gets wrong

- **It cannot see muscle.** A muscular 80 kg athlete at 1.75 m reads as
  "overweight" (BMI 26.1) despite low body fat. Weightlifters routinely
  "fail" the test.
- **It ignores fat distribution.** Visceral fat around the waist carries more
  risk than the same weight carried elsewhere; waist circumference adds
  information BMI cannot.
- **It is not for everyone.** The adult bands do not apply to children
  (who use age-and-sex growth charts), pregnant women, or the very elderly,
  where weight loss can be the bigger risk.

## A healthy way to use it

Treat BMI the way a smoke alarm works: cheap, always on, and good at saying
*look closer* — not at telling you what's burning. A sensible routine is:

1. Check BMI once every few months, not daily.
2. Track the trend, and pair it with waist measurement.
3. If the number drifts out of range — or sits "normal" while your waist
   grows — talk to a professional about the full picture: diet, activity,
   sleep and blood markers.
