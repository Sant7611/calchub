Buying a car in Nepal is, for most people, a financing decision first and a
purchase decision second. With new car prices running into tens of lakhs and
banks typically financing 70–80% of the invoice value, the number that
actually decides affordability is not the sticker price — it is the monthly
EMI.

This guide shows you exactly how EMI is calculated, walks through a complete
worked example with realistic Nepali figures, and points out the inputs that
move the number the most. At the end, you can run your own figures through the
free [Loan Calculator](/tools/finance/loan-calculator).

## What EMI actually is

EMI stands for **Equated Monthly Installment** — the fixed amount you pay the
bank every month until the loan is fully repaid. Every installment contains
two parts:

1. **Interest** on the outstanding balance (large at the start), and
2. **Principal repayment** (small at the start, growing every month).

Because the interest portion shrinks as you pay down the balance, the same
monthly amount slowly shifts from "mostly interest" to "mostly principal" —
this is called amortization.

## The formula

The standard amortization formula for a fixed-rate loan is:

`EMI = P × r × (1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)`

Where:

- **P** is the principal (the amount you actually borrow, not the car price),
- **r** is the **monthly** interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100), and
- **n** is the number of monthly installments (years × 12).

The most common mistake is plugging in the annual rate directly. The formula
needs the monthly rate — for an 11% loan that is 0.11 ÷ 12 = **0.009167**.

## A worked example with Nepali numbers

Suppose you are buying a car invoiced at Rs. 42,00,000. The bank agrees to
finance 80%, so your down payment is Rs. 8,40,000 and the loan amount is:

`P = Rs. 33,60,000`

Assume an 11% annual rate (typical for auto loans from commercial banks in
Nepal in recent years) and a 5-year tenure:

- r = 11 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.009167
- n = 5 × 12 = 60 months
- (1 + r)ⁿ ≈ 1.7289

`EMI = 33,60,000 × 0.009167 × 1.7289 ÷ (1.7289 − 1) ≈ Rs. 73,050`

So the bank takes roughly **Rs. 73,050 every month for 60 months**. The total
you repay is 73,050 × 60 = **Rs. 43,83,000**, which means the interest cost of
the loan is about **Rs. 10,23,000** — nearly a third of the car's price.

In lakh-style grouping (the en-IN locale used across this site for Nepal and

offers.
</Callout>

## The five inputs that move your EMI

- **Loan amount, not car price.** A bigger down payment shrinks P and cuts
  interest for the entire tenure, not just the first year.
- **Interest rate.** On this example, moving from 11% to 12.5% pushes the EMI
  to roughly Rs. 75,600 — over Rs. 1.5 lakh more interest across the life of
  the loan. Always compare the *effective* rate including processing fees.
- **Tenure.** Stretching 5 years to 7 years lowers the EMI but raises total
  interest sharply, because interest compounds over more months.
- **Fixed vs. floating rate.** Many Nepali auto loans are floating, repriced
  against the bank's base rate. Ask how often the rate reviews and what your
  EMI would be at base rate + 3%.
- **Fees.** Processing fees (often ~1%), insurance requirements and prepayment
  penalties change the *effective* cost even when they do not change the EMI.

## The takeaway

The EMI formula is simple; the discipline is in comparing *total interest*,
not just the monthly figure. Two offers with nearly identical EMIs can differ
by lakhs over the life of the loan. Run both through the calculator, look at
the interest total, and let that decide.
