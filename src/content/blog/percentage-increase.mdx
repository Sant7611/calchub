Salary raises, rent hikes, inflation reports, price changes at the shop —
almost every money story is told in percentages. Yet the percentage-increase
calculation trips people up constantly, usually in one predictable way. This
guide gives you the formula, three worked examples, and the mistake to avoid.

## The formula

`Percentage increase = ((new value − old value) ÷ old value) × 100`

Read it out loud and it becomes obvious: *how much did it grow, as a fraction
of what it started from?*

## Example 1 — a rent hike

Your rent goes from **Rs. 25,000** to **Rs. 28,000**:

1. Difference: 28,000 − 25,000 = **3,000**
2. Divide by the old value: 3,000 ÷ 25,000 = **0.12**
3. Multiply by 100: 0.12 × 100 = **12%**

Your rent increased by 12%.

## Example 2 — a salary raise

A salary moves from **Rs. 60,000** to **Rs. 66,000**:

1. Difference: 66,000 − 60,000 = **6,000**
2. 6,000 ÷ 60,000 = **0.10**
3. × 100 = **10%**

A "6k raise" sounds vague; a 10% raise is instantly comparable to inflation
or another offer.

## Example 3 — when the change is negative

Fuel drops from Rs. 170 to Rs. 153 per litre:

1. Difference: 153 − 170 = **−17**
2. −17 ÷ 170 = **−0.10**
3. × 100 = **−10%**

A negative result is simply a **percentage decrease**. The same formula covers
both directions — you don't need a second one.

<Callout title="The mistake almost everyone makes">
Always divide by the **old** value — the starting point — never the new one.
In the rent example, dividing 3,000 by 28,000 gives 10.7%, which understates
the hike. The base of comparison is where the change *started*.
</Callout>

## The reverse calculation

News stories often give you the percentage and hide the numbers: "prices rose
8%". To recover the new price from an old price:

`new value = old value × (1 + percentage ÷ 100)`

So a Rs. 500 grocery basket after an 8% rise costs 500 × 1.08 = **Rs. 540**.

## Compound increases are not additive

Two 10% raises in a row are not 20%. After the first, your base has grown:

`100 → +10% → 110 → +10% → 121`

That's 21% overall. This is the same compounding logic behind inflation over
multiple years and the reason multi-year figures should always be checked
year by year — or with a calculator.

## The takeaway

One formula covers every rise and fall: difference ÷ old value × 100.
Divide by the starting number, keep the sign, and remember that repeated
percentages compound rather than add. Master that and every "X% increase"
headline becomes a ten-second check.
