A budget is simply a plan for where your money should go **before it
disappears**.

That sounds obvious, but many people budget backwards: spend throughout the
month, then see whether anything remains for saving.

A useful budget does the opposite.

`Income − Planned spending = Money assigned before you spend it`

You do not need complicated spreadsheets or dozens of categories to begin.

## Start with take-home income

The first number in your budget should usually be the money you can actually
use after mandatory deductions.

For an employee, that generally means **take-home pay**, not gross salary.

Suppose your monthly take-home income is:

**Rs. 60,000**

That Rs. 60,000 is the amount you need to distribute between expenses,
savings, debt payments and personal spending.

If your income changes each month, use a conservative average rather than
building a budget around your best month.

## Separate needs from wants

The simplest useful distinction is between three groups.

### Needs

Expenses that are difficult to avoid:

- housing
- basic food
- electricity
- water
- transportation
- essential healthcare
- minimum debt payments
- basic communication costs

### Wants

Things that improve your lifestyle but are not essential for basic living:

- restaurants
- entertainment
- subscriptions
- travel
- upgraded electronics
- hobbies
- non-essential shopping

### Savings and financial goals

Money used to improve your future financial position:

- emergency savings
- investments
- retirement
- additional debt repayment
- education funds
- major future purchases

Once your expenses are grouped this way, budgeting becomes much easier to
understand.

## The 50/30/20 budget rule

One popular starting framework is the **50/30/20 rule**:

- **50% — Needs**
- **30% — Wants**
- **20% — Savings and debt goals**

The formula is simple:

`Category amount = Monthly take-home income × Category percentage`

For someone earning Rs. 60,000 per month:

### Needs

`60,000 × 50% = Rs. 30,000`

### Wants

`60,000 × 30% = Rs. 18,000`

### Savings

`60,000 × 20% = Rs. 12,000`

So the starting budget becomes:

| Category | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Needs | Rs. 30,000 |
| Wants | Rs. 18,000 |
| Savings / debt | Rs. 12,000 |
| **Total** | **Rs. 60,000** |

The [Budget Planner](/tools/finance/budget-planner) can calculate these amounts
instantly and help you compare your planned spending with your income.

<Callout title="50/30/20 is a guideline, not a law">
Housing, family responsibilities and local living costs vary enormously. If
your needs require 65% of your income, that does not mean you have failed at
budgeting. The percentages are a starting framework that you can adjust to
your real situation.
</Callout>

## A budget must balance

At the most basic level:

`Income = Spending + Saving`

If your planned expenses exceed your income, the budget has a deficit.

For example:

- Income: Rs. 60,000
- Planned expenses: Rs. 65,000

Monthly shortfall:

`60,000 − 65,000 = −Rs. 5,000`

That Rs. 5,000 must come from somewhere — savings, borrowing, credit, or
reduced spending.

A budget makes that problem visible before it becomes debt.

## Fixed and variable expenses

Another useful way to organize expenses is by how much they change.

### Fixed expenses

Usually similar each month:

- rent
- loan EMI
- insurance
- tuition
- internet subscription

### Variable expenses

Change depending on behaviour or usage:

- groceries
- electricity
- fuel
- eating out
- entertainment
- shopping

Variable expenses are usually the easiest place to make small adjustments
when the budget becomes tight.

## Do not forget irregular expenses

A common budgeting mistake is to plan only for bills that arrive every month.

Some expenses are predictable even though they are not monthly:

- annual insurance
- vehicle maintenance
- festivals
- school fees
- gifts
- medical costs
- travel
- device replacement

Suppose you expect an annual expense of Rs. 24,000.

Instead of being surprised later:

`Rs. 24,000 ÷ 12 = Rs. 2,000 per month`

Set aside Rs. 2,000 each month.

This is sometimes called a **sinking fund**.

## Build an emergency fund

A budget should not assume that every month will go perfectly.

Unexpected expenses happen:

- medical bills
- repairs
- temporary unemployment
- family emergencies
- urgent travel

An emergency fund provides a buffer so those events do not immediately become
high-interest debt.

The correct target depends on your circumstances, but the important first step
is simply creating a regular emergency-saving category.

## Saving should not be "whatever is left"

Consider these two approaches.

### Approach 1

`Income − Spending = Savings`

You spend first and save whatever remains.

Often, very little remains.

### Approach 2

`Income − Savings = Spending allowance`

You decide the saving amount first and build spending around what remains.

This is commonly described as **paying yourself first**.

Even a small automatic amount can create more consistency than waiting for a
perfect month.

## What if your expenses are too high?

If the numbers do not fit, start with the biggest categories.

Reducing a Rs. 20,000 expense by 10% saves much more than worrying about a
Rs. 100 purchase.

A useful order is:

1. Review major fixed expenses.
2. Remove unused subscriptions.
3. Set realistic limits for variable spending.
4. Compare debt interest rates.
5. Automate savings where possible.
6. Review the budget regularly.

The goal is not to remove every enjoyable expense.

A budget that makes life miserable is unlikely to survive very long.

## Needs and wants are personal

Some expenses cannot be classified perfectly.

A phone may be essential for work but the newest premium model may be a want.

Transport may be essential, while choosing a more expensive vehicle may
contain both a need and a lifestyle choice.

Budgeting works better when you are honest with yourself rather than trying
to force every expense into a perfect definition.

## Review the plan monthly

Your first budget will probably be wrong.

That is normal.

At the end of the month, compare:

`Planned amount vs Actual amount`

Then ask:

- Which category was underestimated?
- Where did I overspend?
- Did an irregular expense appear?
- Did I meet my saving target?
- Does next month's plan need adjustment?

A budget becomes useful through repeated correction.

## The takeaway

Budget planning is not about restricting every purchase.

It is about deliberately deciding where your income should go.

Start with your take-home income, identify needs and wants, assign money to
savings and future goals, and make sure total spending does not exceed the
money available.

The 50/30/20 framework is a useful starting point, but your real budget should
reflect your own income, living costs and priorities.
